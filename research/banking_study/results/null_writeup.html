The question

Cohen, Malloy & Nguyen (Journal of Finance, 2020) show that when firms change the language of their annual filings, the market underprices it: a portfolio long the non-changers and short the changers earns 34–58 basis points a month. They measure how much text changed, using term-frequency similarity across the whole document.

This study asked a narrower question. If 86% of textual changes are negative in sentiment — their figure, which we verified in the paper — then the informative subset should be deletions specifically. Does an LLM that classifies each risk disclosure as retained, reworded, softened, removed or absorbed extract a cleaner signal than counting changed words?

US regional banks, FY2019 through FY2023, spanning the 2023 crisis. Item 1A (Risk Factors) and Item 7A (Market Risk) pulled from EDGAR, split into disclosure units, aligned with dense embeddings, and classified by a language model. Forward returns measured against KRE from the day after each filing.

The semantic classifier — the thing the project exists to do — is insignificant at every horizon and points the wrong way at twelve months. Plain word-counting beats it on every cut. The two signals correlate at −0.42, so they overlap partially; most of what the LLM adds is not what predicts returns.

The one lead, and how it died

Item 1A similarity at twelve months gave +7.98%, t = 2.48 — correctly signed, magnitude matching the paper's ~7% a year, and the only specification in the study to clear significance. It looked like a finding.

It was drawn from 24 specifications. Shuffling returns against signals 1,000 times asks how often chance alone produces a t that large somewhere among 24 tries:

Spearman's rho across the whole distribution is +0.099 — no monotone relationship. And in the crisis cohort, the subsample the entire thesis is about, the spread reverses to −23.42%: among banks that actually got into trouble, quiet filings preceded worse returns, not better.

Direction without significance. Positive in all four years, robust to dropping the crisis banks and to winsorising — yet indistinguishable from noise once the search is accounted for. This is what an underpowered test of a small real effect looks like. It is also what a null looks like. This design cannot tell them apart.

Why it could never have worked

The null was structural, and knowable before a single filing was parsed:

Observations needed to detect it~725

Roughly a third of the sample required. Worse, US banks nearly all file in February, so the 224 observations cluster into four independent time periods, not 224 independent draws — inside a window dominated by one sector-wide shock. More banks would not fix this. More years would.

What the instrument was actually doing

The more useful output of this study is not the null. It is the catalogue of ways a pipeline can produce confident, plausible, wrong output — none of which was visible from its results.

Embeddings
Never ran. A stale auth token made the hub answer 401 for an optional file; the loader silently fell back to bag-of-words TF-IDF while every document, endpoint and export continued to claim dense semantic alignment.
Thresholds
13% of records never saw the judge. A recall cut calibrated for dense cosine similarity, applied to bag-of-words scores, auto-labelled 4,346 units as NEW with a hardcoded confidence of 0.85. Re-running one bank with real embeddings took its NEW rate from 28% to zero.
Corpus
Cohort was confounded with code version. The corpus was ingested over five days while seven pipeline commits landed, and each cohort happened to be processed on a different day. Unit yield tracked the ingestion date, not the issuer.
Conclusions
Hardcoded. The generated report asserted a strong negative relationship while the computed spread was flat-to-inverted. The three "key findings" were string literals that could never contradict the data.
Alignment
Look-ahead control never engaged. A key-name mismatch meant every observation fell back to a hardcoded filing date. Four distinct dates across 240 rows — the study's headline methodological claim, silently inert.
Judge
7% precision on its most-used signal class. Measured against blind labels: RETAINED 94%, ABSORBED 86%, REMOVED 50% with conservative errors — but SOFTENED, weighted equally in the score, was wrong nine times in ten.
Item 7A
Unusable. Extracted length ranges from 20 to 52,602 words, bimodal between cross-reference stubs and over-captures that swallow neighbouring sections. This is the section holding the quantitative sensitivity tables the thesis depends on.
Every one of these produced output that looked correct. Nothing crashed, no run reported failure, and the numbers had the right shape. They were found by auditing the instrument against its own claims, not by reading its results.

What would answer the question

  1. Extend the window, not the universe. FY2010–2023 gives roughly fourteen independent periods instead of four. This is the binding constraint; nothing else moves it.
  2. Validate the judge before scaling it. A hand-labelled gold set exists and is scored; SOFTENED needs to be fixed or dropped from the score before more data is bought.
  3. Fix or abandon Item 7A. Many banks cross-reference market risk into MD&A and have no standalone 7A. That is a design decision, not a bug fix.
  4. Control for M&A. The paper excludes merger windows explicitly; this study never did, and mergers dominate the top removal scores.

The tool itself stands apart from all of this. Show me what this company stopped saying does not require the anomaly to replicate in 57 banks over four years.
